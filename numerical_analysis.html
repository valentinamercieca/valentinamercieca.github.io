Numerical Analysis

The Numerical Analysis module focused on the mathematical and statistical methods that underpin data science and artificial intelligence. It used R to put those methods into practice, with an emphasis on choosing an appropriate method, interpreting the results and communicating what the evidence supports.

Unit 1: Types and Sources of Data and an Introduction to R

This unit revisited the distinction between descriptive and inferential statistics and explored how the source of a dataset influences the conclusions that can be drawn from it. We looked at nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio scales and how they guide the choice of summary. For example, a frequency table can summarise responses on an ordered scale without assuming that the gaps between categories are equal.

Formative Activity: Descriptive Statistics in RStudio

I used the 2013 to 2014 Crime Survey for England and Wales teaching dataset to summarise respondents’ scores on the antisocx measure of neighbourhood antisocial behaviour. I imported the SPSS file into R using haven, checked for missing values and used summary() and psych::describe() to examine the centre, spread and shape of the available scores.

The mean was close to zero and the distribution was right-skewed, with a tail of higher scores. Looking back, I would check how the survey defines the scale before interpreting what a value near zero means. I would also check how the survey coded missing values. Only 2,149 of the 8,843 records had a value for antisocx, so the results describe the recorded scores, and their representativeness depends on which respondents had a score.

The R script shows how I generated the summaries, and the RStudio activity write-up records my results and interpretation. They can be found here, respectively.

Unit 2: Data Structures in R

This unit explored how R stores and works with data in vectors, matrices and data frames. We looked at arithmetic operations on vectors and matrices and how to inspect variables in a data frame. The survey exercise gave me a practical example of why it matters to check what a variable represents. A column stored as numbers can contain codes for categories, which affects how it should be summarised.

The counts stayed the same after conversion, but the labels made the table easier to interpret.

Unit 3: Data Management in R

This unit built on the previous work with data frames by focusing on how to inspect and prepare imported data. We looked at how to check the structure of a dataset, examine individual variables, convert data types and handle missing values. The survey exercise illustrated why checking value labels matters before filtering. A code such as 7 only identifies the intended age group once its meaning has been confirmed in the dataset.

Unit 4: Probabilities, Probability Distributions, and Sampling

This unit explored how probability describes uncertainty and how sampling affects what we can infer about a population. We looked at discrete distributions for outcomes such as counts and continuous distributions for measurements such as time. We also compared simple random, stratified random, systematic and cluster sampling.

Formative Activity: Probability Exercises

In one exercise, I calculated that two mutually exclusive events with probabilities of 0.26 and 0.37 had a combined probability of 0.63 because they could not occur together. In another, I used independence to show that knowing one event had occurred left the probability of the other unchanged.

Unit 6: Statistical Inference, Confidence Intervals, and Hypothesis Testing

This unit built on the work on probability and sampling by looking at how sample data can be used to draw conclusions about a population. We explored how confidence intervals communicate the precision of an estimate and how hypothesis tests use a test statistic and a p-value to assess evidence against a null hypothesis. The significance level provides a threshold for that decision, while the estimated effect helps put the result into context.

Seminar Preparation: Hypothesis Testing

For the seminar, I used Sarmukaddam (2012) to review the roles of the null and alternative hypotheses and consider how sample size affects inference. A larger sample can improve precision and narrow a confidence interval, while the way the sample is selected still matters for how well it represents the population.

I connected this to my work as a statistician. A difference can be statistically significant and still be too small to matter for the decision at hand. Looking at the estimated size of the difference and its confidence interval helps put the test result into practical terms.

Unit 7: Parametric Tests

This unit built on hypothesis testing by looking at how to choose and interpret parametric tests in R. We covered t-tests and the F statistic used in ANOVA, with attention to how the study design and the assumptions of each test guide that choice. For example, comparing two independent groups calls for a different approach from comparing measurements taken from the same people before and after an intervention.

Formative Activity: Comparing Systolic Blood Pressure in R

I used the Health_Data dataset to calculate the mean, median and mode for systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and income. I also summarised the income distribution and created a boxplot to show its spread.

I then used Welch’s two-sample t-test to compare mean systolic blood pressure between participants with and without a peptic ulcer. The test gave a p-value of 0.2296, and the 95% confidence interval for the difference in means ran from −2.89 to 11.80. At the 5% significance level, the result provided insufficient evidence of a difference in mean systolic blood pressure between the two groups. The confidence interval also showed the range of differences compatible with the data under the test assumptions.

Unit 8: Nonparametric Tests

This unit built on the parametric tests covered in Unit 7 by examining when rank-based tests are appropriate. We compared the Mann-Whitney U test for two independent groups, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired observations and the Kruskal-Wallis test for multiple independent groups. The choice of test depended on the type of data, how the observations were collected and what the analysis aimed to compare.

Formative Activity 1: Comparing Blood Pressure Across Groups

I used the Health_Data dataset to summarise participants’ ages and explore differences in blood pressure. After checking normality with the Shapiro-Wilk test, I used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare diastolic blood pressure by diabetes status and the Kruskal-Wallis test to compare systolic blood pressure across occupational groups. The p-values were 0.7999 and 0.8545, respectively, so both tests provided insufficient evidence of a group difference at the 5% significance level.

Unit 9: Creating Cross-tabulations and Performing Chi-Square Analysis

This unit explored how cross-tabulations summarise the relationship between two categorical variables. We also looked at how to create subsamples in R and how a chi-square test of independence compares observed counts with the counts expected if the variables were independent.

Formative Activity: Cross-tabulating Crime Victimisation by Age Group

I used the 2013 to 2014 Crime Survey for England and Wales teaching dataset to cross-tabulate whether respondents had experienced crime in the previous 12 months (bcsvictim) by age group (agegrp7). I used table() to count responses and prop.table() to calculate row percentages, then formatted the results to two decimal places with knitr::kable().

Looking back, the denominator matters for interpreting this table. The row percentages show how respondents in each victimisation category were distributed across age groups. To identify which age groups had the highest and lowest proportions of respondents who reported experiencing crime, I would calculate column percentages and compare the values in the victim row.

Unit 10: Correlation

This unit explored how correlation describes the strength and direction of an association between two variables. I focused on interpreting Pearson’s correlation alongside a scatter plot. The coefficient summarises the linear association, while the plot helps reveal features such as unusual observations or a curved pattern that a single number can obscure.

Formative Activity: Correlation Between Systolic and Diastolic Blood Pressure

I used the Health_Data dataset to calculate Pearson’s correlation between systolic and diastolic blood pressure in R. Using cor() with use = "complete.obs", I included only records with both measurements. The correlation was approximately 0.847, indicating a strong positive linear association. In this dataset, participants with higher systolic readings tended to have higher diastolic readings.

I also used ggplot2 to create a scatter plot, with systolic blood pressure on the horizontal axis and diastolic blood pressure on the vertical axis. This gave me a way to inspect the pattern behind the coefficient.

The Unit 10 R script containing the correlation calculation and scatter plot code can be found here.

Unit 11: Regression Analysis

This unit built on correlation by introducing simple linear regression. In Unit 10, I used Pearson’s correlation to summarise the association between systolic and diastolic blood pressure. In this unit, I used a fitted regression line to estimate diastolic blood pressure from systolic blood pressure and considered what the slope, p-value and (R^2) each tell us about the model.

Formative Activity: Predicting Diastolic Blood Pressure in R

I used lm() to fit a simple linear regression model to the Health_Data dataset, with systolic blood pressure as the predictor and diastolic blood pressure as the outcome. The fitted slope was 0.496, meaning that a systolic reading 1 mmHg higher corresponded to a predicted diastolic reading about 0.50 mmHg higher. The slope was statistically significant at the 5% level, and the model accounted for 71.7% of the variation in diastolic blood pressure in the data used to fit it.

Unit 12: Bayesian Data Analysis

This unit introduced Bayes’ theorem and how Bayesian analysis combines prior information with observed data to update what we know about an unknown quantity. I considered how a prior can make existing knowledge explicit, and why it is useful to check how sensitive the conclusions are to the choice of prior.
