The Data Professional

The Data Professional module brought together the technical and professional aspects of working with data. I explored how data is collected, managed and prepared for analysis, and drew on my work at Malta’s National Statistics Office to reflect on confidentiality and responsible data handling. In the two assessments, I designed and implemented a Python analysis of transport survey data from Wales. Using charts and an ArcGIS map, I examined differences in transport satisfaction and access to services, and considered what the survey could support when making recommendations for public transport.

Unit 1: Introduction to Data Science

This unit introduced the role of a data scientist and the skills needed to turn large volumes of data into useful information. We explored how big data, machine learning and artificial intelligence are connected and how they can support scientific discovery and decision-making in industry.

We also considered the implications of big data for privacy and governance. The first collaborative discussion gave me a way to explore these issues through cybersecurity, where data can help identify threats and may itself contain sensitive information.

Collaborative Learning Discussion 1: Data Science, AI and Cybersecurity

In my initial post, I explored how statistics and machine learning can help identify unusual patterns in large datasets and support threat detection (Hero et al., 2023; Kotenko et al., 2020). I also considered the privacy risks of collecting data from connected devices and how manipulated data can undermine the reliability of detection models (Biggio et al., 2012; Xi, 2020).

In my three peer replies, I explored different aspects of responsible data use. In one reply, I considered how giving too little weight to security when making data accessible can expose an organisation to breaches and damage customer trust. In another, I connected data protection requirements to decisions made during a data science project and asked for practical examples of threat detection. In the third, I discussed the importance of data quality, privacy and accountability as the use of connected devices grows.

In my summary post, I brought these points together by considering how Master Data Management can support consistency and accountability throughout the data lifecycle. I also reflected on a peer’s point about keeping safeguards proportionate to the risks involved. The discussion helped me connect the reliability of data-driven security tools with the way organisations manage and protect the data those tools depend on.

Unit 2: Data Management Lifecycle (DML)

This unit introduced the stages of the data management lifecycle, from collection and storage through to use, retention and disposal. Looking at the lifecycle as a whole helped me see how decisions made at one stage affect the next. For example, collecting more personal data than a task requires means there is more information to secure, manage and eventually dispose of.

We also examined how the GDPR shapes decisions about personal data and how the ISO 27000 series provides a framework for managing information security. The examples of data breaches and the growing use of data centres highlighted the importance of knowing where data is held, who can access it and how it is protected throughout its lifecycle.

Seminar Preparation: Advancing Digital Transformation in Financial Services

To prepare for the Unit 3 seminar, I reviewed Advancing Digital Transformation in Financial Services (Intellas, 2023). In my notes, I examined how legacy systems can leave customer data scattered across platforms, making it harder for banks and insurers to see how customers interact with them across different channels. I also considered how regulatory requirements, third-party risks and resistance to change can complicate efforts to modernise those systems.

I explored how digital experience platforms could improve data visibility and make the customer experience more consistent. My notes also considered ways to manage the transition, including consolidating data, encouraging continuous learning and choosing technology that addresses a specific business problem. This exercise helped me connect the data management lifecycle to a practical challenge: bringing data together so it can be used more effectively while managing who can access it and how it is protected.

Unit 3: Introduction to Python Programming Language

This unit revisited familiar programming concepts such as functions and logical operators in the context of Python. I already understood how functions help organise reusable code, so my focus was on how to apply that knowledge in Python and use Jupyter Notebook to run code in sections and inspect intermediate results.

The introduction to NumPy connected these programming concepts to data analysis. Working with arrays showed how the same calculation could be applied to a set of values in one operation, which provided a foundation for the data preparation and analysis covered later in the module.

Unit 4: Data Representation Using Database Concepts

This unit explored how relational databases organise data and maintain consistency across related tables. We looked at how SQL is used to define tables, manipulate records and retrieve information for reporting. The bus depot database provided a practical example of how relationships between drivers, buses, routes and depots determine what can be retrieved and how the results should be interpreted.

SQL Exercises: Bus Depot Database

In the first exercise, I used filters to select records and joined tables to retrieve information about drivers, cleaners, buses and depots. One example was comparing an INNER JOIN with a LEFT JOIN when listing cleaners and their depots. The LEFT JOIN kept cleaners who had no depot assignment in the results, with NULL in the depot fields. This showed how the choice of join affects which records appear in a report.

In the second exercise, I used a subquery and a join to answer the same question about routes associated with Holloway Depot. I also used aggregate functions to calculate average driver salaries by depot and HAVING to report only depots with more than one driver. These exercises gave me practice translating a reporting question into a query and checking that the result represented the intended group.

The first and second SQL exercises can be found here and here, respectively.

Unit 5: Advanced SQL

This unit built on the SQL covered in Unit 4 by combining joins, built-in functions and grouped queries. The focus shifted from retrieving individual records to producing summaries that answer reporting questions, such as how many employees work in each department.

A useful distinction was where to apply a filter. WHERE determines which individual records contribute to a summary, while HAVING determines which groups appear after the summary has been calculated. For example, a report could count active employees in each department and then show only departments with at least five active employees. Thinking through those two conditions separately helped me see how to check whether a query answers the intended question.

Unit 6: An Introduction to Algorithms and Basic Programming Concepts

This unit focused on how to describe a solution before implementing it in code. We looked at how pseudocode and flowcharts can make inputs, decisions and outputs explicit. The unit also covered how to evaluate an algorithm by checking that it produces the expected result, handles different inputs and uses computational resources efficiently.

Formative Activity: Sorting Cards

For this activity, I wrote step-by-step instructions for inserting a new card into an already sorted hand. I used a worked example with a card valued at 4 to illustrate how to find the insertion point between 3 and 5 and check that the hand was still sorted afterwards. The exercise showed how a familiar task can leave decisions implicit. Written instructions need to specify which cards to compare and when to stop.

I connected practices at the NSO with the GDPR, the Malta Statistics Authority Act and the confidentiality principles in the European Statistics Code of Practice.

In my three peer replies, I considered how pseudonymisation and privacy impact assessments can help manage risks to sensitive data. I also discussed how organisations can adapt their policies to the way staff actually work, and how training and oversight of third-party vendors can support data protection in practice.

In my summary post, I brought these ideas back to the data management lifecycle. I discussed how collecting only the data needed, restricting access and using pseudonymisation can protect sensitive information at different stages. I also considered how privacy impact assessments can help identify risks early and how security monitoring can support incident response. Comparing examples from different organisations helped me see how the same data protection principles translate into different day-to-day practices.

Unit 7: Data Collection Using Python

This unit explored where organisations get their data and how Python can be used to collect it. We considered data generated within a business, published datasets and proprietary sources that require permission to access. We also looked at web scraping and APIs as ways of retrieving data online. The format of the data and the way it is made available help determine which collection method is appropriate.

I ran the pandas and NumPy exercises in Jupyter Notebook, building on the Python work from earlier units. The exercises connected the collection stage to what happens after the data is loaded. With pandas, I could inspect how a dataset was organised, while NumPy supported calculations on arrays. This provided a practical starting point for the data preparation covered in the next unit.

Unit 8: Data Pre-Processing

This unit built on the data collection work from Unit 7 by focusing on how to prepare raw data for analysis and machine learning. We looked at how missing values and the way categories are represented can affect the results. One point I took from this unit was that preprocessing choices depend on what the data represents. For example, assigning numbers to categories can imply an order that has no meaning in the original data.

Formative Activity: Data Pre-Processing in Python

In my Python exercises, I used pandas to load CSV files and scikit-learn to fill missing values in selected travel-time columns with their respective means. I also compared integer labels with one-hot encoding. The first approach assigned a number to each category, while the second represented categories in separate indicator columns.

The final exercise used train_test_split to set aside a portion of the data for evaluating a model on examples it had not seen during training. Looking back at my script, I would split the data before fitting the encoder so that the transformation is learned from the training data alone.

Unit 9: Data Analysis and Visualisation Principles

This unit explored how visualisation can support exploratory data analysis and help communicate findings. We looked at static, interactive and geospatial visualisations. I focused on how the question guides the choice of visual. A distribution plot can show how values are spread, while a map can show where a measure varies geographically.

Python Exercises: Data Analysis and Visualisation

I used Seaborn to explore the Titanic dataset, looking at passenger counts by class, patterns in survival and the distribution of ages and fares. I also used Plotly to create an animated scatter plot comparing systolic and diastolic blood pressure over time and a choropleth map to show how average systolic blood pressure varied by country. These exercises gave me practice using different visuals to examine distributions, relationships and geographical patterns.

Assessment: Data Analytics Report

For this assessment, I reviewed the 42 tables in the 2013 to 2014 National Survey for Wales transport results and designed an analysis to support decisions about public transport. I used a UML diagram to show relationships between the selected tables and drew on the data management pipeline and Master Data Management principles to explain how I would prepare and combine them.

I focused on whether respondents had use of a car and how that related to transport satisfaction and ease of reaching services such as GP surgeries and hospitals. I used bar charts to compare survey responses across groups and a line plot to show reported ease of reaching healthcare services among respondents with access to a car. The age of the survey was an important limitation. The results describe respondents’ experiences at the time and would need to be supplemented with more recent evidence to inform decisions about current transport provision.

The Data Analytics Report contains my rationale for selecting the tables, designing the analysis and presenting the results. The report can be found here.

Unit 10: Unified Modelling Language

This unit focused on using Unified Modelling Language (UML) to represent system requirements during analysis and design. We looked at how use case diagrams show what a system needs to do and who or what interacts with it. An actor could be a person, an organisation or an external system. Identifying each actor and what they need to achieve helped me see how a diagram can clarify requirements before development begins.

I connected this to the UML diagram in my Unit 9 transport report, which showed relationships between the survey tables I selected. A use case diagram answers a different design question by showing how an actor would interact with a system. For example, it could show how a transport analyst would retrieve survey results for a local authority. Both examples helped me see how choosing the right diagram makes it easier to discuss a design with the people who will build or use it.

Unit 11: ArcGIS Storytelling

This unit focused on using ArcGIS Online to explore geographical patterns and communicate findings through interactive maps. I worked through the ArcGIS activities and considered how a map and charts can answer different questions about the same dataset. In the Cardiff crime example, a map could show where incidents were recorded, while a bar chart could make it easier to compare the number of incidents by crime type.

The ArcGIS Cascade material also showed how maps, charts and commentary can be presented in a sequence that guides the reader through an analysis. I applied the mapping aspect in my final transport assignment, where I used ArcGIS to show how reported satisfaction with the transport system varied across local authorities in Wales. The map gave readers a way to compare areas before looking at the survey charts on car access and access to services.

Unit 12: Emerging Technologies in Data Management and their Impact - Blockchain

This unit introduced blockchain as a way of maintaining a shared record across multiple participants. We looked at its role in cryptocurrency and considered other applications in financial services, supply chains and the public sector. One question this raised for me was when a record that is difficult to alter would be useful. It could help participants verify a history of transactions, but it could also make correcting errors or removing sensitive information more complicated. The unit also prompted me to consider the environmental costs of running different blockchain systems.

End of Module Assignment: Data Analytics Implementation

For the final assignment, I implemented the analysis I had designed in Unit 9 using the 2013 to 2014 National Survey for Wales transport results. I used pandas to clean, reshape and merge the selected survey tables, and documented how they related to one another using a UML diagram. I then used Matplotlib and Seaborn to compare transport satisfaction and car access across groups. I also included an ArcGIS map to show how reported satisfaction varied by local authority.

One result I discussed was that respondents with access to a car reported lower satisfaction with the transport system than those without car access. I also examined how easily respondents with access to a car could reach GP surgeries and hospitals. That chart described the experiences of one group, so a direct comparison with respondents without car access would be needed to assess differences in healthcare accessibility.

I considered options such as expanding rural public transport and subsidising fares. More frequent services could improve access, while fare subsidies could reduce costs for passengers. The report also considered the trade-offs, including the cost of maintaining subsidies and the limited benefit of cheaper fares where services remain infrequent. Working through the implementation helped me connect decisions about data preparation and visualisation to what I could reasonably recommend from the survey.
